# Decided

What has been decided for mac-os-agent and built. [planned.md](planned.md) has what comes next, and [ideas.md](ideas.md) has experiments.

- The agent has no name of its own
  - The owner does not want it anthropomorphized. It has no personal name, persona, logo, avatar, or greeting, and there is no setting for one. Running text calls it "the agent", and the app, the repo, the bundle identifier, the menu bar item, the folders, the Keychain service, and the sign-in's client name are `mac-os-agent`.
  - The default editable instructions tell it that it is the agent in the mac-os-agent app. They are fixed text, so nothing about them changes from one request to the next. The fixed parts, in the main panel and the incognito window, do not name it either.
  - The panel's top bar shows the app's name, `mac-os-agent`.
  - Earlier builds had a Name setting. A name stored by one, under `agentName` in the preferences, is ignored and left in place. Instructions an owner saved keep their text as written, a name in it included.
- One thread
  - Every summon continues the same thread. There are no separate chats to pick from.
  - The thread is a single append-only chain that goes back in time, divided into episodes. An episode can be one exchange or many.
  - Within an episode the context only grows by appending, so prompt caching keeps working.
- Episodes
  - A new episode starts when the owner sends a message after the last one has been idle longer than a timeout, or when they type `/new`. The timeout defaults to 30 minutes. Idleness counts from the last message, reply, or command in the episode, not from when the panel was shown.
  - A new episode is recorded as an `episode_start` line in `thread.jsonl`, written just before its first message. Nothing earlier is rewritten. The part of the thread written before episodes existed has no markers, and its episodes are derived from pauses of 30 minutes or more. That pause is fixed, so episode numbers never change.
  - Only the current episode is sent to the model, after the instructions and the tool list. A new episode starts with that short prefix, which is where the prompt cache starts over. Earlier episodes are not summarized into the prompt.
  - The fixed part of the instructions tells the agent that earlier episodes exist and are not in view, and to search them when the owner refers to something from before.
- Search over past episodes
  - `search_history` finds the owner's messages, the agent's replies, and the commands that ran, by words, and returns the best matches with their dates, their episode, and the messages around them. It leaves out the episode the agent already sees. `read_episode` reads one episode in order, a page at a time, with the start of each command's output.
  - The search runs on a full text index, `history-index.sqlite` in Application Support. It is a copy of the thread that is brought up to date on launch and rebuilt from the thread whenever it is missing or does not match, so deleting it is always safe. Without it, search reads the entries directly.
  - The index is brought up to date or rebuilt in the background, off the main thread, so launch and the first summon never wait on it. Until it is ready, search reads the entries directly. Entries added meanwhile go into the index when it is ready, each once.
- Incognito
  - Incognito is a window of its own, separate from the main panel. `/incognito` in the main panel, or Open Incognito Window in the menu bar menu, opens it. When it is already open, both bring it forward with its text field focused. There is one at a time. The main panel does not change: it keeps its thread, its episode, and its focus rules, and both windows can be on screen and used at once.
  - Both windows are the same panel and the same content view with different configuration: the same top bar, history, rows, text field, inspector, and theme.
  - It leaves no trace. Its conversation runs on a store held only in memory, with no file and no index. Nothing from it reaches the thread, the search index, `instant-actions.json`, the preferences, or any other file. Only token counts reach `usage.jsonl`, which holds no content. Its problems go to a log of its own that nothing shows and that goes with it.
  - It is only for opening apps and asking questions. The agent there has the three application tools, `search_applications`, `open_application`, and `list_running_applications`, and nothing else: no shell, no Reminders or Calendar, no system tools, no clipboard, no file search, no skills, no themes, no tools from MCP servers, and no history search or episode reading. It cannot search the kept past.
  - Its instructions are the owner's editable instructions followed by a short fixed part of its own, which says what the window is, what it can and cannot do, and to say plainly when a request needs the main panel. They are read once when its conversation starts. Its tool list and instructions stay the same for the whole conversation, and its requests use a prompt cache key of their own, so they are a cached prefix apart from the main panel's.
  - It stays open until the owner clears it. It has no episode timeout. Closing it, with its top bar's close button, Close and Discard in its top bar's menu, or Command-W, discards the conversation. Clear in its top bar, or `/new`, discards the conversation and keeps the window open on a fresh one. There is no way to hide it without discarding. With no task running, closing and clearing happen at once. With one running, a card by the text field asks first, and Escape or Keep answers no. Quitting the app discards it too.
  - Escape never closes it. It dismisses an instant action suggestion, or the inspector, or a question about closing, and otherwise does nothing, so a reflex press cannot lose the conversation.
  - `/incognito` typed inside it sends nothing and shows a line saying it is already incognito.
  - The hotkey and the menu bar icon toggle only the main panel.
  - It is plain which window is which. The incognito window's surface has the theme's incognito tint for its whole life, and its top bar says `mac-os-agent` followed by "Incognito. Nothing here is kept." Its top bar's menu has no layouts, only Close and Discard.
  - It opens beside the main panel, to its right when there is room, then to its left, and otherwise against the screen's edge on the roomier side. It is 480 by 500 points, and the owner can move and resize it. Nothing about its place is remembered between uses, since that would be a trace.
  - Instant actions work there and open apps with no model request. It offers no quick actions, as it has no skills. The window stays up after one. It ranks matches by what the main panel has learned, read when the window opens, and it learns nothing and writes nothing.
- Commands in the panel
  - A message that is exactly a known command, such as `/new`, is handled by the app. It is never sent to the model or written to the thread. Any other text that starts with a slash is sent as a normal message.
  - The commands are `/new` and `/incognito`. Typing a slash lists the commands that match, under the history. In the incognito window `/new` clears its conversation and `/incognito` only says it is already incognito.
- Skills as slash commands
  - In the main panel, typing a slash lists the app's commands and then every skill the owner can invoke, by its name with its description as the hint, filtered by prefix as they type. While a skill's text is typed, only that skill stays listed. Up and Down, and Shift-Tab, choose. Tab or Return puts the chosen command in the field, and so does a click. A command typed in full runs on Return.
  - Escape hides the list for that draft, then closes the inspector, then hides the panel, as it does for an instant action suggestion. A slash rules out a suggestion, so they share the space above the text field and only one shows.
  - A skill can be invoked unless its front matter has `user-invocable: false`, for a skill meant only for the agent. That is the key Claude Code uses. The Agent Skills format has none. Any other value is a problem with the file. Off, broken, and agent-only skills are not listed. Settings says in each skill's detail how it can be invoked, or why it cannot.
  - The app's commands win over a skill with the same name, such as a skill named `new`. That skill is not listed or invokable with a slash, Settings says why and suggests renaming it, and the agent can still load it.
  - `/skill-name some text` sends one message: the typed line, then a note from the app and the skill's instructions between `<skill>` tags, the same text `load_skill` returns. It saves the `load_skill` round trip, and it is a new message at the end, so the instructions, tools, and everything earlier in the episode go out exactly as before and the prompt cache holds. Everything after the name, over any number of lines, is the skill's text, which may be empty.
  - The thread stores the typed line as the message's `text`, and the message the model was sent in an optional `skill` field beside it, with the skill's name. The panel, history, and search show the typed line. Lines without the field load and are written as before, and nothing stored is rewritten. A later request in the episode sends the stored message, whatever has happened to the skill since.
  - A skill that was turned off, broke, or went away since the list was read is not sent. The draft stays and a line says why.
  - The list is live. The app watches the skills folder and each skill's folder, as it watches the themes folder, and reads them again when one changes, when Settings turns a skill on or off or makes one, when a tool call finishes, and when the panel is summoned. A skill the agent writes, one made in Settings, and one changed in Finder show without a relaunch.
  - When a task ends in which a skill the owner can invoke was added or changed, a quiet line by the text field says so, such as "/rename-photos is ready to use." or "/rename-photos is updated.", and the list already has it. It is shown only, never kept.
  - The incognito window has no skills, so it lists and runs only the app's commands. Text there that starts with a skill's name goes to the model as written.
- Instant actions
  - A short input that is confidently an action is answered on the spot with no model request. Applications are the only kind so far: "saf", "safari", "open safari", and "switch to slack" open the app. Anything else goes to the agent exactly as before, so the model is the fallback and never a second guess.
  - The check runs on every keystroke against the installed apps, read ahead of time from the standard folders and never from Spotlight. It takes well under a millisecond, so it runs in step with the text and never shows a suggestion for text that has since changed.
  - The rule is strict, because opening an app when the owner meant to send a message is worse than being slow.
    - The whole input is a few words at most, with no line breaks and no sentence punctuation, optionally after a launch verb: open, launch, start, switch to, go to, or bring up.
    - A word such as "the", "is", "it", or "and" makes it a sentence, unless the input is exactly an app's name, such as Find My.
    - Without a verb, a match has to be the app's name, a prefix of it, a whole word of it, a word prefix, or initials from three letters. Typos and letters found inside a word are left to the agent.
    - A single everyday word, such as "find", "help", "word", or "what", opens only an app with exactly that name, or with a verb an app with it as a whole word ("open word").
    - A single character opens only what the owner took for that character before. With the suggestions-in-the-field experiment on, a single character suggests, below.
    - The best match has to lead the next clearly to be offered alone. Otherwise the few close ones are offered to choose between, but only with a verb or three or more characters.
  - A quiet row above the text field, where the command hint shows, says what Return will do. Return opens the app, clears the field, and hides the panel the normal way. Command-Return sends the text to the agent instead. Up, Down, Tab, and Shift-Tab choose between several matches. Escape dismisses the suggestion for this input, and the next Escape closes the inspector or hides the panel as before. The suggestion stays dismissed while the owner keeps typing after it.
  - Nothing about opening an app this way is sent to the model or written to the thread, and the panel shows no line for it, so the episode's request and its prompt cache stay as they were. With the panel with bars experiment on, the main panel logs the opening and shows a line for it, and it joins the next request lazily. See Experiments. If the app fails to open, the panel comes back with the text and a line saying why, which is shown only.
  - What the owner opens this way is learned, in `instant-actions.json` in Application Support: which app they took for which typed text, and how often and how recently each was taken. It ranks the matches, so "s" can come to mean Safari for one owner and Slack for another. The incognito window learns nothing and writes nothing.
  - The model's `search_applications` and `open_application` tools are unchanged.
  - Quick actions are the second kind, below. Providers for other kinds, such as System Settings panes, recent files, web addresses, or skills, can be added behind the same rule and row later.
- Quick actions
  - "Just do it." A quick action is a stored tool call, or a short run of them, with the arguments filled in. It is data the app runs, not prose the model reads, so it runs like opening an app: the closest thing to invoking something in Alfred. Skills stay as they are, for tasks that need judgment.
  - Each is a JSON file in `quick-actions/` in Application Support, named by its id, such as `quick-actions/start-screensaver.json`, in the shape of the theme files: `"format": 1`, a `name`, a one-line `description`, optional `phrases`, `enabled` (on when left out), and `steps`, each a `tool` and its whole `arguments` object. At most 10 steps and 12 phrases. A file that cannot be read is listed in Settings with its problem and never offered. Unknown keys are ignored.
  - The folder was `actions/` until quick actions were renamed from saved actions. At launch, when `actions/` exists and `quick-actions/` does not, the app renames it, once. When both exist it touches neither, uses `quick-actions/`, and says so in Debugging and the log, naming the folders and nothing in them.
  - Typing a quick action's name or one of its phrases offers it in the instant row, beside the apps, by the same rule and the same learned use in `instant-actions.json`. A tie goes to the quick action. Its name may start with a launch verb ("Start Screensaver"), so it is matched against the whole input as well as the part after a verb, and "run" is a verb for it. The row shows the name, a short reading of the steps in their tools' own words, such as "Open ScreenSaverEngine", and "↵ runs". Command-Return still sends the text to the agent.
  - Return runs the steps in order through the same tools the model's calls go through, with no model request, and stops at the first step that fails. Any tool works with no code per action. The file is read again when it runs, so one turned off or broken since the row showed it does not run, and a line says why.
  - No go-ahead is asked. Choosing the quick action from a list is the go-ahead, so every go-ahead a step would ask for is given at once, including a shell command that `DestructiveCommandCheck` matches. The check still says why such a step would have asked: Settings shows it under the step, `change_quick_action` says it when the agent saves one, and the run's record keeps it. Refusals that are not a go-ahead, such as a built-in theme that cannot be deleted, still stop a step.
  - Each run is kept, unlike opening an app. The typed text and a record of each step, its arguments, its output, and how it ended go into the thread as the owner's message, in an optional `action` field beside `text`, after an episode marker or answers to unanswered calls as a typed message would be. The model is sent a note between `<action_run>` tags in place of the typed text, stored whole like a skill's message, so when the owner follows up ("undo that") the agent knows what ran. A note written before quick actions were renamed still says "saved action" and is sent as it was. It is a message rather than tool calls, since a call with no model turn that made it is not valid input, and it is appended at the end, so the cached prefix holds. An older build reads the line as a plain message.
  - The panel shows the typed text, a row per step as it runs, which the inspector opens like any call, and an error line when a step failed. With the panel with bars experiment on it is one line instead. See Experiments. The main panel hides the normal way and comes back with the text in the field when a step fails.
  - The agent manages quick actions with `read_quick_actions`, which lists them and the format or shows one file, and `change_quick_action`, which saves a whole file or deletes one. A save is checked in full: the file's shape, and each step against its tool's JSON Schema (types, required keys, enums, items, and no extra keys). A quick action cannot call those two tools. They were `read_actions` and `change_action`, in the same place in the tool list. Calls under the old names already in the thread are sent to the model as they were, and their rows read as the renamed tools'. Saving needs no go-ahead, since nothing runs until the owner picks it, and deleting asks. The two tools add about 1,100 characters to every request.
  - The fixed instructions say what a quick action is, in those words, so the model uses the owner's name for it, to offer to save one when a request came down to a fixed call or calls the owner is likely to repeat, or when they ask to "make that instant", and to use a skill for tasks that need judgment. They also say what an `<action_run>` record is.
  - The list is live. The quick actions folder is watched, as the skills folder is, and read again when a tool call finishes and when the panel is summoned. When a task saves or changes one, a quiet line by the text field says so, such as "The quick action Start Screensaver is ready to use. Type its name and press Return."
  - Settings has a Quick Actions section of its own, after Skills. Its line at the top reads "Just do it.", with a sentence under it on what a quick action is. Then a list with a filter, and each quick action's name, file, description, what it is typed as, its steps with their tools and arguments, an on and off switch, Open File, Show in Finder, and Delete.
- Its own harness
  - The agent loop is ours: a while loop and tool calls, talking to the model API directly.
  - No dependency on pi or any other coding agent. Pi is an influence on the design, not a component.
  - The agent has no working directory, project, or session directory. Those are coding tool concepts.
  - The harness is Swift, inside the app, so there is no second runtime to ship.
- OpenAI first, behind a seam
  - The loop talks to models through a small provider interface, with one implementation per provider.
  - The first and only provider for now is OpenAI, using "Sign in with ChatGPT" and the Responses API. There is no API key to manage.
  - The app registers as its own client at sign-in. It does not reuse the Codex CLI's client or the ChatGPT backend.
  - Only a release build signs in. A development build, one with no update feed, has no sign-in of its own and sends every model request, to OpenAI and to Ollama, through inference-gateway at `http://127.0.0.1:47811`, which holds the sign-in for the owner's apps (MAC-34). A run with `--data-folder` follows the build. The rule is `ModelAccess` in `MacOSAgentCore`.
    - Requests to the gateway carry no credentials and name the app in `X-Client-App: mac-os-agent`. They are otherwise the same requests, with the same session headers, on the same paths after `/openai` or `/ollama`.
    - When nothing answers there, the error says inference-gateway is not running and that its launchd agent, `dev.lubelski.inference-gateway`, starts it. When it has no sign-in, the error shows its own message, which says what to run.
    - The menu, Settings General, and Debugging say the models go through inference-gateway, and show no sign-in controls.
  - It follows OpenAI's documented path for open-source and locally hosted apps, at https://developers.openai.com/siwc/token-sharing-open-source. That path registers its own client at sign-in, with no requested client ID, client secret, or partner API key. OpenAI's interest form is only for paid or remotely hosted apps.
  - The first sign-in registers with `dynamic_agent_client` and the name hint `mac-os-agent`. The client ID OpenAI issues is kept with the account it was issued for, the `sub` and email of its ID token, and every later sign-in reuses it: the issued client ID, no name hint, the same host ID, the email as `login_hint`, and a retained ID token as `id_token_hint` when there is one.
    - A reused sign-in that comes back as another account is refused, and nothing is changed. A callback that names a different client ID is refused too.
    - When OpenAI rejects the reused client ID, with `invalid_client` or `unauthorized_client`, the app drops it and registers afresh in the same sign-in, which opens the browser a second time.
    - The issued client ID is kept as soon as the browser returns, before the code is traded, so a failed exchange does not cost a second registration.
    - Settings, General has Use a Different Account beside Sign In while signed out with a kept registration. It registers anew and replaces the kept registration. The app keeps one registration, so going back to the first account registers again.
    - An installation from before this kept the client ID and the refresh token only. Its next sign-in reuses that client ID and records the account, with no step from the owner.
    - The ID token's issuer, audience, expiry, and nonce are checked. Its signature is not yet checked against OpenAI's published keys.
  - Signing out removes the refresh token and the ID token at once and keeps the registration. Then it revokes the refresh token at the `revocation_endpoint` named in OpenAI's OpenID configuration. A network failure or a server error is tried again, three attempts in about three seconds. When no attempt is confirmed, an alert says the owner is signed out on this Mac only and to disconnect the app in ChatGPT Settings to be sure, and Debugging shows it. No token is ever logged or shown.
  - The models offered are the signed-in account's own list, from `GET /v1/models` with the same access token: the ones with `visibility` "list", in the order OpenAI gives. It is read in the background after each sign-in and at launch when signed in, and nothing waits on it. Until it arrives, when signed out, and when it cannot be read, the menu and Settings offer the fixed list in `OpenAIModel.all`. A development build has no sign-in, so it never reads the list and always offers `OpenAIModel.all`.
  - A saved model the account's list leaves out stays selected. It is offered after the listed ones, Settings says it is not in the account's list, and the menu bar menu marks it.
  - The default model is `gpt-6-luna`, the fastest GPT-6 model, with low reasoning effort. The model is a setting in the menu bar menu and in Settings, and both read the same preference.
  - Every model is sent "low" reasoning effort, including models from the account's list the app has never seen. It is the lowest effort every known model accepts, and the request body is the same as before for every model.
- Its own tools
  - The agent runs with a set of tools that it can modify itself.
  - The tool list sent to the model stays fixed within a conversation, because a list that changes breaks prompt caching.
  - Built-in tools are a fixed, ordered list. New built-in tools go at the end. Tools from MCP servers go after the built-ins, sorted by name.
  - The built-in tools are a shell command, three app tools (search the installed apps by a loose name, open an app or bring it to the front, and list the running apps), a tool that loads a skill, two tools for past episodes (search them, and read one), two tools each for Reminders and Calendar (read, and change), six system tools (open an item, find files, read and write the clipboard, set the desktop picture, and quit or hide an app), two appearance tools (read the themes, and save, apply, or delete one), two quick action tools (read the quick actions, and save or delete one), two memory tools, and, only when Brave Search is chosen for web search, `search_web` after them. With the panel with bars experiment on at launch, `set_links` comes last of the built-in tools. With the settings tools experiment on at launch, `find_tools` and `use_tool` come after it, last of the built-in tools. With the scripts experiment on at launch, `run_script` comes after every other tool, the MCP tools included.
- Settings
  - A Settings window with a sidebar of sections on the left and the selected section beside it, in the style of System Settings. The sections are General, Appearance, Instructions, Skills, Quick Actions, Tools, MCP Servers, History, Behaviors, Experiments, and Debugging. MCP Servers shows only with the MCP servers experiment on, and a remembered section that is hidden opens General instead.
  - A title bar spans the whole window, above the sidebar and the section, with the section's name centered in it and a hairline under it. The sidebar is 200 points wide and cannot be dragged or collapsed.
  - The window keeps one size whichever section is showing. It opens at 900 by 620 points, the owner can resize it down to 760 by 480, and it comes back at the size and place it was left. A section scrolls inside the window when it does not fit. The last section shown opens again next time.
  - Debugging is always the last section. New sections go above it. Experiments sits just above Debugging, and Behaviors above Experiments.
  - General holds the sign-in, the model, web search, the episode timeout, the summon shortcut, and updates. The timeout is a choice from 5 minutes to 8 hours, 30 minutes by default. The model picker lists the account's models once they are read, and says so.
  - History lists the kept episodes newest first, with each one's date, length, and first line, and a search over all of them that lists the episodes that match with the matching line. The selected episode is shown read-only, with the owner's messages, the commands that ran, and the replies as selectable text. Incognito conversations are never kept, so they never appear. Nothing can be edited or deleted there yet.
  - Skills is a list of every skill, with a filter, beside the selected skill's detail: its description, on or off, its problem if the file is broken, the files in its folder, and its `SKILL.md` for reading. Editing happens in the owner's editor.
  - Tools lists the agent's tools in the order the model sees them, grouped by where they come from, with each tool's description as sent, its parameters, whether it asks for a go-ahead, and whether the incognito window has it. Tools from an MCP server are a group of their own, one per server. A built-in tool's detail is read-only. An MCP tool's detail has one control, whether it asks before each call.
  - With the pane stack experiment on, Tools is a stack of panes instead. See "Settings panes that slide over" under Experiments.
  - A group of tools that needs a macOS permission, such as Reminders or Calendar, shows in each tool's detail whether the app has it, with a button that opens that pane of Privacy & Security, and one that asks for it when the owner has not been asked yet.
  - Debugging is read-only. It shows recent model requests with their token counts and duration, the prompt's size and fingerprint, the sign-in state and where the sign-in is kept, the thread file's size and entry count, problems since launch, and the build and how it is signed. Copy Diagnostics puts the same as plain text on the clipboard, without message content or secrets.
- Themes
  - The panel draws from one theme at a time. Settings keeps the standard system look and is not themed.
  - A theme is a JSON file, format 1: a name, and a light appearance, a dark one, or both. A theme with only one appearance holds the panel to it. Each appearance gives the surface (Liquid Glass, a frosted blur, or a solid fill, with an optional tint whose alpha is its strength, and an opaque fallback fill), the primary, secondary, and tertiary text, the accent, the fill and edge of the owner's message box, the agent's reply box, and the go-ahead card, the divider, the color of the bracket around code, and the incognito tint and badge. An appearance may also give `bar`, the fill of the panel's bars. It is optional, and without it the bars are the primary text at 3 percent, so files written before the key existed read and draw as before. It may also give `field`, a fill and an edge for the text field drawn as a pill with the panel with bars experiment on. It is optional too, and without it the pill is the primary text at 6 percent with an edge at 12. Every built-in theme sets its own, so the pill is plain on the dark themes and High Contrast. Text in the pill is checked for contrast over its fill laid on the bars' fill. Colors are `#RRGGBB` or `#RRGGBBAA`, or a system color name such as `system-label 13%`, which the Glass theme uses to keep the system's look and vibrancy. Fonts, layout, and corner radii are not themeable.
  - Six themes are built in: Glass, the default, which looks exactly as the panel did before themes, Tidewater, a frosted teal blur, Graphite, a solid dark gray, Paper, a warm light page, High Contrast, in light and dark, and Ember, a warm brown glass. Built-in themes cannot be changed or deleted.
  - The owner's and the agent's themes are files in `themes/` in Application Support, one per theme, named by id, such as `themes/night-swim.json`. A new or changed file shows without a relaunch. A file that cannot be read is listed in Settings with its problem, and the panel falls back to Glass when the active theme's file breaks or goes away.
  - The active theme and the Use glass switch are kept in the app's preferences.
  - Use glass in Settings turns glass and blur off for every theme, which then draws on its fallback fill. Reduce Transparency in Accessibility settings does the same, whatever the switch says.
  - Increase Contrast strengthens a theme's box fills by a third and its edges and divider to at least 35 percent, and an edge the theme leaves empty takes its box's fill at full strength.
  - Primary and secondary text must reach 4.5:1, WCAG AA, everywhere it sits: on the surface, on the bars, in the text field's pill, in each box, and on the incognito label. A glass or blur surface is checked over its fallback fill and with its tint over pure black and pure white. Text in a system label color is held to 3:1, the level the system's own secondary label reaches, and on glass only over the fallback fill, since vibrancy adapts it. The built-in themes are checked by a test.
  - Appearance in Settings sits under General. It shows each theme as a small live preview of a few rows of conversation, built-in themes first, then the owner's and the agent's, with the active one ringed. Clicking one applies it to the panel. It can duplicate a theme, show the themes folder in Finder, delete one of the owner's themes, and turn Use glass off.
  - The agent makes themes with `read_themes`, which lists the themes and the format or shows one theme's file, and `change_theme`, which saves a whole file, saves and applies it, applies a theme, or deletes one. A save is checked in full, so nothing malformed reaches the folder, and text that is too hard to read is refused with each pair of colors that fails and by how much. Deleting asks for the go-ahead. The two tools add about 1,140 characters to every request.
- Measuring prompt caching
  - The app reads the token counts from each model response: input, cached input, output, and reasoning. They pass through the provider seam in a provider-neutral shape.
  - Each model request adds one line to `usage.jsonl` in Application Support with the time, provider, model, and counts. It holds no message content and is kept apart from the thread.
  - Each line also records how long the request took and a fingerprint of the instructions and tool list sent with it, so a change that restarted the cache shows up.
  - Debugging in Settings shows the recent requests and today's total, with the cached share as a percentage.
  - A line also records the replayed reasoning items the model reported withholding, counted by reason, from the response's `input_transformations`. `prefix_mismatch` means input before the item changed since the model wrote it, which is also where the prompt cache broke. `turn_boundary` is left out, since older models drop earlier turns' reasoning that way every time. The field is missing when nothing was withheld.
  - Each request sends `session-id` and `x-client-request-id` headers holding the prompt cache key, as pi does on the same endpoint, so OpenAI routes the thread's requests to the same cache.
  - A line also records the tokens the request wrote to the cache (`written`, from `cache_write_tokens`), and short digests of what it sent: one for the tool list, then one per input item (`items`). `diverged` is the index of the first digest that differs from the request before it in the same conversation and model, and is missing when the request before it is a prefix of this one. It is kept in memory, so the first request after a launch has none to compare with, and the digests still allow the comparison by hand. Digests hold no content.
  - A line says which Mac and build wrote it: `device`, the first 8 characters of the installation id, `machine`, the hardware model, and `build`, the app's version. The agent runs on more than one Mac, and their logs are compared.
  - Tests drive the real loop and OpenAI provider against a stand-in for the Responses API, with a fake prompt cache that follows OpenAI's published rules for GPT-5.6 and later, through one episode with a tool call, a skill message, a relaunch, and a model change. They check that each request starts with the one before it, that each reads the previous one's prefix from the cache, and that no replayed reasoning is withheld. The episode's requests are kept in `Tests/MacOSAgentCoreTests/Fixtures/openai-wire-episode.json`, so a change to what goes over the wire shows as a diff.
- Instructions the owner can edit
  - The system prompt has two parts. The editable part is a file, `instructions.md` in Application Support, and the default applies until the owner saves an edit.
  - A fixed part always follows it. It covers what the app needs to work correctly: how the destructive flag and the go-ahead work, the time at the start of each message, that earlier episodes exist and how to search them, and how skills are found, loaded, and written. The incognito window has a fixed part of its own instead, described under Incognito.
  - The prompt is read once at the start of each instruction from the owner, so an edit applies from the next one. It comes out byte for byte the same when nothing changed, so the prompt cache holds.
  - Settings shows the editable part and the full prompt in the system's monospaced font. The editor saves what is typed: smart quotes, smart dashes, text replacement, and autocorrection are off in it.
- Memory
  - The agent keeps what it learns about the person from one episode to the next, such as their home address or how they want a thing done. It manages memory itself. Settings, Memory shows it for inspection and debugging.
  - It is a folder, `memory/` in Application Support: `core.md`, plain Markdown with what the agent needs in most episodes and every standing rule or prohibition; topic files, `<name>.md` with `name` and `description` front matter as a `SKILL.md` has, for what is long or only matters for one subject; `archive/`, where a deleted topic file goes instead of being destroyed; and `journal.jsonl`, the history.
  - The memory block goes at the very end of the main panel's instructions, so everything before it stays a cached prefix: core, then an index of the topic files that the app makes from their front matter, with the day each last changed. The agent never writes the index.
  - The block is frozen per episode. It is taken when the episode's first request is built and kept, in memory, for the rest of the episode, so the instructions stay byte for byte the same. A change made partway through is already in the conversation as the tool call, and joins the block at the next episode. A relaunch partway through an episode takes the block again, which costs one cache miss.
  - Caps: core about 4,000 characters, core and the index together 8,000. After each write the tool's result gives core's size, and says to move long or topic-specific items into a topic file when core is at 80% or over. Over 8,000 the index is cut first, keeping the files changed most recently, then core is cut at a line, keeping its start. A line says what was left out and that `read_memory` reads the rest.
  - Two tools at the end of the fixed tool list, after every other tool: `read_memory` (no file for core and the whole index uncapped, or one file by name) and `change_memory` (create, replace, remove, append, set_description, rename, delete). Edits are local: replace and remove need text that matches exactly once, and in a topic file they touch only the body. Delete archives. None asks for a go-ahead, since every change is journaled and can be restored.
  - The fixed instructions say memory is the agent's to manage; save what the person asks it to remember and lasting facts, not what only matters to the task; standing rules go in core, since a topic file is seen only when read; read a topic file when its description matches; check before writing and update rather than duplicate or contradict; move items out of core as it fills; never store passwords, card numbers, or other secrets, even when asked, and say why; and "forget that" removes it, with a brief word in the reply.
  - The journal is append-only JSON Lines, written as the thread file is. Each change adds an entry with the date, the file's path, the operation, the file's full content after it (nil when the file left that path, with the names for a rename, archive, or move out of the archive), a hash of the content, and the cause: the episode, Settings, or outside the app. An archive adds two entries, one for each path. Nothing is ever removed. A restore writes an old revision back as a new entry.
  - A change made outside the app, in an editor or Finder, is found by comparing each file's hash with the last one journaled. The app checks at launch, when the folder changes, and before each change of its own, and journals what it finds as edited outside the app.
  - Reading the journal reads the whole file. A full copy per change stays small for a long time. If it grows past a few megabytes: keep the last hash per path in a small side file so a check does not read everything, and start a new journal file per year, read only for history.
  - After a turn that changed memory, the panel shows one notice line, such as "Remembered in core.", "Updated travel-plans.", "Forgot something in core.", or "Forgot old-trips." Only the agent's changes count.
  - Settings, Memory comes after Instructions, and Debugging stays last. It lists the block as frozen for the current episode, core with its size against the cap, the topic files with description, size, and date, and the archive. For a file it shows the text, raw editing (journaled as a Settings change), Show in Finder, archive and move back, and the history from the journal, where any revision can be viewed and restored. It is kept live by a folder watch.
  - Memory contents are never logged. Debugging and the usage log show only counts and sizes, and the full prompt in Debugging shows the block as its size.
  - The incognito window has no memory block and no memory tools.
- Skills
  - A skill is a folder in `skills/` in Application Support with a `SKILL.md` that has `name` and `description` front matter and then instructions, plus any scripts beside it. This is the Agent Skills format.
  - The prompt lists only the name and description of each skill that is on. The agent reads the rest with the `load_skill` tool when a task needs it.
  - The owner can turn skills on and off, make a new one, and open the folder from Settings. Skills that are off are listed in `skill-settings.json`, so a new skill is on from the start.
  - The agent can write and improve skills itself with the shell tool. This is how it extends what it can do. A new or changed skill is listed in the prompt from the owner's next instruction, and in the panel's slash commands at once.
  - The fixed part of the instructions tells the agent that the owner can invoke a skill with a slash, and that `user-invocable: false` keeps one for the agent only.
- Apps through native tools
  - The app tools use Launch Services, NSWorkspace, and Spotlight directly instead of the shell, because shell lookups with `mdfind` were slow and missed apps.
  - A search reads the standard app folders, caches each folder until it changes, and asks Spotlight only when nothing matches closely.
  - Opening, searching, listing, and hiding apps need no go-ahead. Quitting an app goes through `quit_or_hide_application`, which asks first and quits the way the app's Quit menu item does, so it can ask to save.
- Reminders and Calendar through native tools
  - `read_reminders`, `change_reminders`, `read_events`, and `change_events` call EventKit from inside the app instead of `osascript`, which was slow, fragile, and needed Automation permission for each app.
  - Each area has one tool to read and one to change. A change tool takes a list of changes, so the model can do several in one call, and each change creates, edits, or deletes one item.
  - The app asks for full access to Reminders or to Calendar the first time a tool needs it. When access is denied or restricted, the tool says so and names the pane in System Settings that grants it. The bundle carries `NSRemindersFullAccessUsageDescription` and `NSCalendarsFullAccessUsageDescription`.
  - Deleting, changing more than one item in a call, and changing a repeating event from one occurrence on ask for the same go-ahead as a destructive shell command. Creating, completing, and editing a single item do not ask.
  - Dates go in as ISO 8601. A date alone is a day: due that day, or an all-day event. A date-time without an offset is in the Mac's time zone. Results write dates as `Sat 2026-11-21 14:00` in the Mac's time zone and say which zone that is. A reminder with a due time also gets an alert at that time, as the Reminders app does.
  - Each item comes back with a short id, eight characters from a hash of EventKit's identifier, so it is the same every time it is read. A repeating event's id adds when that occurrence was scheduled.
  - The tools sit behind small protocols, so their logic is tested against stand-in data and never against the owner's real reminders and events.
- System features through native tools
  - `open_item` opens a web address, file, or folder with its default app or a named one, or shows it in Finder, through NSWorkspace. `find_files` searches with Spotlight by name, content, kind, and date, newest first, in the home folder unless told otherwise, and leaves out hidden files and `~/Library`. `read_clipboard` and `write_clipboard` use NSPasteboard. `set_desktop_picture` sets an image file on every screen. `quit_or_hide_application` quits or hides a running app.
  - They use public frameworks only and need no new permission. Anything that would need a new permission class, private API, or the shell is left out of this set.
  - Only quitting asks for a go-ahead. Writing the clipboard and setting the desktop picture do not, since the owner asked for them and they are easy to put back.
- Web search
  - One setting in Settings, General, with three values: Off, OpenAI, and Brave Search. Off is the default. It is kept in the preferences under `webSearch`, by `WebSearchSetting`. One provider at a time.
  - Off leaves the app as it was: the same tool list, request body, prompt, and fingerprint. A test holds this.
  - The setting is read once at launch, since it changes the tool list or the request, which stay fixed for the run. Settings says "Takes effect the next time mac-os-agent launches." when the stored choice differs from the one the run launched with.
  - OpenAI is OpenAI's hosted web search, the tool Codex uses. The request adds `{"type": "web_search"}` after the function tools, so the list before it is unchanged. The model searches on OpenAI's side and no call comes back to the app. It reaches the provider through a provider-neutral `webSearch` flag on `ModelRequest`, and the fingerprint in `usage.jsonl` changes when it is on.
  - Each finished `web_search_call` item is kept whole in the thread as a provider item, as reasoning is, and sent back on later requests of the episode to the same model, as Codex does, since `store` is false. Older lines load unchanged and nothing stored is rewritten.
  - The panel shows each search as a tool row in the usual style: `Search the web <query>`, `Open page <url>`, or `Find <text> in <url>`. The row appears while the search runs, from the item the stream adds, and the finished item fills it in. A row rebuilt from the thread reads the same. It opens in the inspector with the action, the status, and sources when the item has them. It never asks for a go-ahead. Citations stay as links in the reply's Markdown. There is no citations view.
  - If the API refuses the tool for the sign-in, the usual error line shows its message. There is no fallback.
  - Brave Search is a built-in tool, `search_web`, in the list only when Brave Search was chosen at launch. It goes at the very end of the built-in list, after the memory tools, so every tool before it keeps its place. It is not a quick action step. Its group in Settings, Tools is Web Search.
  - It calls `GET https://api.search.brave.com/res/v1/web/search` with the key in `X-Subscription-Token`, and takes `query`, `count` (1 to 10, 5 when null), and `freshness` (`pd`, `pw`, `pm`, `py`, or null). The model reads a numbered list of titles, addresses, descriptions without Brave's highlight markup or HTML entities, and ages, cut at 16,000 characters. No results, no key, a refused key, the rate limit, another status, a network failure, and the 15 second time limit are each one plain sentence. Its row reads `Search the web <query>`, like the hosted search, and it never asks for a go-ahead.
  - Its definition adds 592 characters to every request when it is in the list.
  - The Brave key is a secret, kept where the sign-in is, under `brave-search-api-key`: the Keychain in a release build and `secrets.json` otherwise. It is never in the preferences, the log, or Copy Diagnostics. Settings, General has a secure field with Save and Remove, and shows "Key saved" without ever showing the key. The tool reads the key at each call, so a new key needs no relaunch.
  - The release build's one-time move to the Keychain keys off the sign-in, not any secret, so a Brave key already in the Keychain does not stop a sign-in from moving. A key the Keychain already has keeps its value there.
  - Debugging shows "Web search: off", "OpenAI", or "Brave, key set" or "key not set", and what the next launch uses when that differs. Never the key.
  - The incognito window has no web search of either kind. Its tool list and requests are unchanged.
- MCP servers, first slice
  - All of it is behind the MCP servers switch in Settings, Experiments, off by default. See Experiments.
  - A change to the servers takes effect at the next launch. Hot reload and progressive disclosure are the next slices, in [planned.md](planned.md#mcp-servers).
  - Local servers only: ones the app starts as a process and talks to over stdio. An entry with a `url` or a `type` other than `stdio` is listed with a problem.
  - The client is the official MCP Swift SDK (`modelcontextprotocol/swift-sdk`, 0.12.1), the second dependency after Sparkle, behind a seam of our own, `MCPServerConnection` in `MacOSAgentCore`. Only `MCP/MCPSDKConnection.swift` imports the SDK. The rest of the app, tests, and previews use the seam with the app's own types.
  - The app starts each server's process itself, in a process group of its own, with pipes for its input, output, and error. Stopping a server ends the whole group, SIGTERM then SIGKILL, so the node process `npx` starts goes with it. Quitting the app stops every server and waits up to a second before killing what is left.
  - An app opened from Finder gets launchd's short PATH, so the command is found on a PATH made of what a login zsh sets up (read once, between markers, since a profile may print), then the folders `npx`, `uvx`, and the like usually live in (Homebrew, `~/.local/bin`, `~/.cargo/bin`, `~/.bun/bin`, `~/.deno/bin`, Volta, pnpm, mise, asdf, and the newest nvm node), then the app's own. The server is started directly with that PATH, not through a shell, since a shell's profile could write to the server's output. A command with a slash is used as written. An `env` that sets PATH wins.
  - The servers are listed in `mcp-servers.json` in Application Support, in the `mcpServers` shape other apps use (`command`, `args`, `env`), so an entry can be pasted from one. The owner edits it in their own editor. The app sets it to 0600 when it finds it readable by others, since `env` can hold tokens. Settings shows the names in `env`, never the values.
  - A file that cannot be read is one problem, shown in Settings and the problems list. An entry that is wrong keeps its name and its problem, and the others still load.
  - A server's name is 1 to 32 letters, digits, hyphens, and single underscores, and does not start or end with an underscore. That keeps `<server>__<tool>` within the model's function name rules and keeps `__` meaning only the line between the server and the tool. An entry whose name breaks the rule is listed with the reason and never started.
  - What the app itself decides is kept apart, in `mcp-settings.json`, as `skill-settings.json` is for skills: which servers are off (each is on unless listed), and each tool's go-ahead where the owner set one that differs from its default.
  - Each tool is named `<server>__<tool>`, with the description and JSON Schema the server gave it, and sent without strict mode, since a server's schema is not written for it. The schema gets an object type and an empty properties object when it lacks them. A tool name the model's API would refuse, which allows letters, digits, underscores, and hyphens up to 64 characters, has its other characters made underscores, and a name that is then too long or the same as another's is shortened and given a short hash of the tool's own name, so it stays the same from launch to launch. A name that fits as it is always keeps it.
  - The tool list is still put together once, at launch, and launch never waits for a server. The app keeps each server's last tool list in `mcp-tools-cache.json` and builds the list from the servers file and that cache. Servers start in the background. A call to a tool whose server is not connected yet waits for it, up to 60 seconds, and a server that failed is tried again at the next call. A call that cannot reach its server returns one plain sentence to the model saying why.
  - A new server, or one whose tools changed, has its new list cached when it connects. Its tools join at the next launch, and Settings says so. Turning a server off stops it now, and its tools leave at the next launch. Turning one on starts it now.
  - Go-ahead is per tool, one of three: ask each time, always allow, or always deny. The default asks, except for a tool its server marks read-only (`readOnlyHint`), which is always allowed. The owner changes it with a picker in the tool's detail in Tools, and the tool's row in the list shows a mark for it. The change applies to the next call without a relaunch, since it does not change what the model is sent.
  - A tool set to always deny stays in the model's list, since changing the list would need a relaunch. A call to it never asks and never reaches its server. The model reads that the owner set the tool to always be denied and that only the owner can change it, in Settings > Tools. A script's call to it fails with the same sentence.
  - `mcp-settings.json` keeps always allow as `dont-ask`, its name before always deny existed, and always deny as `deny`. A value a build does not know is skipped, and the tool's other settings still load.
  - The card asks "Call this tool?" and shows the tool, its server, and the arguments as JSON. Its buttons, left to right, are Deny, Always Deny, Always Allow, and Allow Once. Allow Once is the default button, on Return, and Deny the cancel button, on Escape. Always Deny and Always Allow answer this call and save the same setting Tools changes, so the next call does not ask. A line on the card says the Always choices are saved for this tool and can be changed in Settings > Tools. They work the same on a card a script asked for. Only an MCP tool's card has them. There is no choice that lasts only for the conversation.
  - A result's text content is what the model reads, joined by lines. Images, audio, and resources are named in one bracketed note and left out. A result the server marks as an error reads as `Error:` and its text. A result longer than 16,000 characters is cut there, with a note giving its full length. A call that takes more than 120 seconds is stopped, and the model is told.
  - Settings has an MCP Servers section after Tools: the servers in the file beside the selected one's detail, with an on or off switch, its state (connected, starting, failed and why, off, or not valid and why), its command and arguments, the names in its `env`, its tools, a line when its tools join or leave at the next launch, and Open File and Show in Finder. With no file, it says where the file goes and shows a short example. Adding and editing a server happens in the file.
  - A server that fails is reported to the problems list in Debugging, once for each different reason.
  - The incognito window has no MCP tools.
  - `--check-mcp-servers` with `--data-folder` starts the servers in that folder as a launch does, calls an `echo` tool if there is one, stops them as quitting does, writes `mcp-check.txt` there, and quits, with no menu bar item, hotkey, or window. It is for checking a bundled build against real servers.
- Ask before destructive actions
  - A shell command waits for a go-ahead in the panel when the model marks it destructive, or when it matches a broad local pattern list for deleting, overwriting, uninstalling, quitting, killing processes, `sudo`, and system settings.
  - Either one is enough. The model cannot talk its way past the pattern list.
  - The card's buttons are Deny and Allow Once, the same words as an MCP tool's card. It has no Always choices, since the ask comes from the command, not from a setting for the tool.
- A conversation you summon
  - The panel is a small chat window, not a launcher bar. It opens at 640 by 560 points, the owner can move and resize it, and it comes back where it was left, across summons and launches.
  - The whole thread is scrollable history above the text field, read as episodes. Each episode starts with a separator that shows its date and time between hairlines across the history, with the widest gap in the thread above it and a wide one below it, so it reads as a break between episodes and not a mark inside one. Rows are built lazily as they scroll into view, so a long thread scrolls smoothly.
  - When the panel comes up after the last episode timed out, or after `/new`, it opens on an empty page as tall as the history, with earlier episodes above it, out of view until the owner scrolls up. The page has a line saying the earlier episodes are above, and a slot at the bottom for content such as suggestions. Within the timeout it opens on the current episode.
  - Once an episode has its first message, it is only as tall as its rows, which sit at the bottom by the text field with no space left over below them. The episode before shows above, past its separator. A thread shorter than the panel sits at the bottom too.
  - Rows of one exchange sit close together, commands in a run closer still, and each exchange starts a little further down.
  - It opens at the latest message and follows new output unless the owner has scrolled up.
  - The owner's messages and the agent's replies sit in quiet boxes on opposite sides. The owner's have a faint accent tint, and the agent's a neutral tint strong enough to stand apart from the glass. Inside an episode, a time label marks a message on a new day or after a pause of 30 minutes or more.
  - The agent's replies are GitHub Flavored Markdown: paragraphs, emphasis, inline code, fenced code blocks, lists, task lists, quotes, links, headings kept close to the text size, rules, strikethrough, and tables. The owner's messages stay plain text.
    - Foundation's own parser reads it (`AttributedString(markdown:)` with full syntax), and the panel draws it with native, selectable text. There is no Markdown package.
    - A single line break in a reply stays a line break, as before.
    - Code blocks keep their lines and scroll sideways. Tables keep their columns aligned as written, wrap a long column at a fixed width, and scroll sideways inside the reply when wider than it.
    - While a reply streams, its unfinished end is completed before parsing, so it keeps its shape as the rest arrives: an open code fence stays code, a table's header row is a table at once, and open emphasis, code spans, and links are closed or held back.
    - The default instructions encourage Markdown where it helps, including tables for tabular answers, and still ask for short replies.
  - Messages can be copied. A quiet `copy` control shows beside a message under the pointer and copies a reply's Markdown as written, or the owner's text. A code block has its own `copy` control after its label for just the code. A message's context menu has Copy, Copy as Plain Text for replies, Share, and Speak. A command's context menu has Copy Command and Copy Output.
  - The conversation uses no icons and no status colors. Its marks are characters in the text color, at secondary and tertiary levels, taken from the owner's Pi customizations in agent-customizations.
    - A tool call is one small line that starts with `›`, or `✗` when it failed, then the tool's summary: what the call does or the tool's name, then what it acts on, such as `Open Safari` or `Bash du -sh ~/Movies`. Every tool's row is drawn the same way, its first word in the secondary level and the rest in the tertiary. The shell tool's rows say `Bash`, a name only the panel uses: the model knows the tool as `run_shell_command`. Opening the line shows the call in the inspector beside the panel. The line never grows in place.
    - A command that needs words gets one more line starting with `└`, beneath the start of the command: why it failed, that it is running, that it is waiting for a go-ahead, or that it never ran because the owner declined or the task stopped. A command that never ran is struck through.
    - Code and command text is held by an open bracket: `┌──` above, a rule down its left side, and `└──` below. The go-ahead card and the inspector's input use it.
    - Arrows are the characters `↑` and `↓`.
  - It stays light: a floating Liquid Glass panel with no title bar, sidebar, or conversation list. The text field has focus when it appears, Return sends or takes the instant action the row offers, and Escape hides it.
  - The text field holds one icon at its left and the text, and nothing else.
    - The icon is the SF Symbol `sparkle`, the single four-pointed star, not `sparkles`, in the secondary level and centered on the first line of the text. It replaced the `›` prompt mark, which the tool rows keep. With the suggestions experiment on, the icon of what Return will act on takes its place in the same slot, so the text never moves when one replaces the other. One view, `PromptMark`, draws it in the field, on the experiment's Ask row, and in the theme samples in Settings, Appearance.
    - No other control goes in the field. A microphone button for dictation is the one possible exception, and it is not decided. See voice dictation in [planned.md](planned.md).
    - Stop is not in the field. It goes with the block that is working, so while a task runs the last row of the thread is a working row: a small spinner, then Stop, about as tall as a tool row and in line with the replies. The thread keeps it in view as it keeps new rows in view, and it goes away when the task ends or is stopped. It is the thread's only working indicator. The field is full width whether or not a task runs. With the queue experiment on, queued messages stay above the field, below the thread. The incognito window is the same. With the panel with bars experiment on, the working row and the queued messages are drawn as that experiment says, below.
  - A slim bar across the top of the glass moves the panel. It is set apart from the history by the same hairline that sets the text field apart, from one shared definition, `PanelDivider`. The pointer stays the arrow over it, with no open hand.

The top bar and the band with the text field and its mark are drawn on the bars' fill, a faint offset from the surface: the theme's `bar`, or the primary text at 3 percent, lighter on a dark theme and darker on a light one. The owner liked it on the suggestions experiment's footer and asked for the top bar and the field to share it. The conversation keeps the surface. The rows above the field, the queued messages, the instant action row, the command hint, and the go-ahead and discard cards, stay on the surface, above the hairline, so the hairline is where the band starts. The fill is one definition, `PanelBarFill`, which every bar draws, and it is the same with the experiment off or on.

It is clipped by the surface's shape, so it follows the rounded corners and the square ones on a flush side. Text on it is checked for contrast as text on the surface is. High Contrast's bars are barely offset, and its stronger dividers do the separating.

The incognito window goes back to its opening size beside the main panel. The move is immediate, as choosing a preset is.

It does what Hide Panel and Command-W do: the main panel hides and its task keeps going, and the incognito window closes after its discard question. Its room is kept while it is hidden, so the title stays centered and nothing shifts. It is a button whether or not it shows: a click on its spot closes, even if the pointer has only just arrived, and the rest of the bar still drags and resets on a double click. VoiceOver finds it at rest too, with the label Close, or Close and Discard, and help that names Command-W. In the incognito window Clear follows it and always shows. Later the left end is room for an episode title.
  - The bar's right end has one quiet control, an ellipsis, that opens a standard Mac menu: the layout presets Centered, Slide Over Left, and Slide Over Right, with a check on the current one, below a separator Keep on Top, and below another Hide Panel, which does what Escape and Command-W do. In the incognito window the menu has only Close and Discard, since Escape never closes that window. The preset items have no keyboard shortcuts, because the standard ones for placing a window, Fn-Control with an arrow, belong to the system's window tiling. A new preset is one more case of `PanelPreset`, and the menu lists it on its own. The menu leaves the panel key with the text field focused when it closes.
- Layout presets
  - Choosing a preset moves the panel there. The preset lasts through hiding and relaunching.
  - Centered is a floating panel the owner moves and resizes, which appears and disappears in place. Choosing Centered from slide over brings back the frame it had before. Choosing it while already centered puts the panel back at 640 by 560 points in the middle of its screen.
  - Slide over keeps the panel flush against the left or right edge of its screen's visible area, with no gap, at the full height from just below the menu bar to the bottom, at a width the owner resizes from the free edge and that is remembered. With the Dock on that side it is flush against the Dock, not under it. The owner asked for flush over the earlier floating glass with a 10 point margin, which looked unfinished against an edge.
  - The two corners on the side against the screen's edge are square, and the two on the free side keep the panel's rounding, 12 points. The top and bottom corners are not squared against the menu bar or the bottom of the screen. The glass, the clip on its layer that shapes the window and its shadow, and the slide all follow that shape. The shadow is lost on the flush side, where it would fall off the screen, and may show faintly on a display beside that edge.
  - The shape is square on a side only while that side is flush against the screen's edge, by `PanelGeometry.flushEdges`. A panel dragged off the edge has its four rounded corners back at once.
  - Each preset's frame comes from one function, `PanelGeometry.presetFrame`, and `SummonPanel.returnToPresetFrame()`, the same as choosing the current preset again, puts the panel back there.
  - In slide over the panel slides in from its edge when summoned and back out past it when hidden, in 0.3 seconds on one ease out curve. A summon during the slide out turns it around from where it is, and so does hiding during the slide in. The text field takes focus at once, not when the slide ends. Nothing in the panel reflows on the way. With Reduce Motion on, it fades in place in 0.15 seconds instead.
  - The glass slides inside a window that reaches the edge of the screen's visible area, which cuts it off there. So it leaves at the edge and is never seen crossing a display beside that edge, and with the Dock on that side it leaves at the Dock. It slides with Core Animation driven by the display, not by moving the window, because a window cannot be cut off at a screen's edge.
  - Dragging a slide over panel away from its edge makes it Centered where it was dropped.
  - A slide over panel goes back in place on its screen when a display changes resolution or the Dock moves, and on the screen with the mouse when its display is unplugged.
  - The dock state from before presets moves to the matching slide over on the first launch, and the frame from before docking becomes the centered frame.
  - The text field is a standard Mac text view with everything the system gives one: the Edit menu's commands and shortcuts, the usual key bindings, input methods, spelling, text replacement, dictation, and the emoji picker. It grows with its text up to eight lines, then scrolls. Shift-Return and Option-Return add a line.
- The summon shortcut
  - The owner sets it in Settings, General, with a shortcut recorder: click it and press a chord. Escape cancels, Delete or the clear control removes the shortcut, and Restore Default brings back Option-Shift-Space, the default for a fresh install.
  - A change applies at once. The old hotkey is unregistered and the new one registered. If the new one fails, the old one is registered again and the recorder says what happened.
  - While recording, the hotkey is suspended, so pressing it records it, and every key pressed is swallowed, so nothing reaches the menus or the app underneath.
  - A chord needs Command, Option, or Control, except a function key, which works alone. Shift alone, and Option or Option-Shift with a key that types a character, are refused because they type. So are the app's own menu shortcuts, a few that nearly every app has (Command-N, O, S, P, T, F, G, M), and chords macOS keeps for itself (Command-Tab, Force Quit, Lock Screen, Log Out). Menu shortcuts are matched by the character the key types, so they follow the keyboard layout.
  - The hotkey is registered with Carbon's `RegisterEventHotKey`, which needs no Accessibility or Input Monitoring permission, and with its exclusive option, so a chord another app holds exclusively fails instead of going to both. Carbon does not report a chord that one of macOS's own shortcuts uses, so the recorder reads them with `CopySymbolicHotKeys` and warns when one that is turned on matches, such as Spotlight's Command-Space. The shortcut is still set, so it works once the owner turns the system one off.
  - It is stored in the preferences as a trigger, a small JSON value with a kind, today always `keyChord`, a key code, and modifier flags. A cleared shortcut is stored as `none`, so it is not mistaken for a fresh install. Other kinds of trigger, such as double-tapping a modifier, would be new kinds of the same value.
  - Shortcuts are shown with the usual modifier symbols in the usual order, and the key is named by what it types on the current keyboard layout, falling back to the US layout.
  - The shortcut shows in Settings, as the key equivalent of the menu bar menu's Show or Hide item, and in the icon's tooltip. That item follows whether the panel is presented, so during a slide out it already says Show and brings the panel back. If it fails to register at launch, the menu says so, Settings and Debugging show the problem, and the menu bar icon still toggles the panel.
  - The recorder is our own code, about two hundred lines, rather than a package. The usual package, KeyboardShortcuts, keeps shortcuts in a storage shape of its own that has no room for other kinds of trigger, and its public interface does not tell the app when a registration fails, which is the case the owner most needs to see. The app has no dependencies so far, and this did not justify the first.
- The tool call inspector
  - Opening a tool call row, with a click or with Return or Space when the row has keyboard focus, shows the call in an inspector beside the panel. There is one inspector. Opening another call, or Up and Down while it is open, swaps what it shows. Up and Down work in the inspector, and in the text field while the field is empty. The call it shows has a faint fill in the conversation, at the same weight.
  - It is a child window of the panel, not a wider panel. The panel's frame, layout preset, saved frame, and resize limits stay as they are, and AppKit moves the inspector, orders it, and takes it across Spaces with the panel. It has the same glass, becomes key only when the owner clicks into its text, and has a close control. Escape closes it first, and hides the panel on the next press. The incognito window has an inspector of its own, which closes when its conversation is cleared.
  - It is as tall as the panel and sits on the side with room: the panel's free side, which is the right, or the left in slide over at the right, and the other side when the free side has no room for 320 points. It keeps a 10 point margin from the screen's edge, though the panel itself is flush. On a screen too narrow for either, it covers the panel's far side from the screen's edge and leaves at least 280 points of the conversation in view. The owner can resize its width, which is remembered. Which side it takes is one function, `PanelGeometry.inspectorPlacement`.
  - In slide over the inspector slides with the panel as one unit, the way out long enough for both to pass the edge, and is cut off at the same edge.
  - At the top are the call's facts: the tool and its group, the status (running, waiting for a go-ahead, succeeded, failed, declined, stopped, timed out), when it started and how long it took, a shell command's exit status, the go-ahead and its answer, the output's size in lines and characters and whether it was shortened before the model saw it, and the call id. A running call counts its time live.
  - Each tool result keeps a few more facts for this, beside the output: when the tool started, how long it took and how much of that was waiting for a go-ahead, the go-ahead's answer, the tool's group, a command's exit status, whether it timed out or was stopped, and how long the output was before it was shortened. They are optional fields on the result's line, so older lines load unchanged and show less, and nothing earlier is rewritten. The model never sees them, so requests and the prompt cache are unchanged.
  - The input is laid out for reading. A shell command shows in full and wraps, and a long one breaks before each `|`, `&&`, `||`, and `;` outside quotes, substitutions, and groups. Other tools show their arguments as named fields in the model's order, and an array of flat objects, such as the change tools' `changes`, as numbered records without their null fields. A raw view shows the arguments as written.
  - The output is laid out by its shape, with fixed rules and no model: JSON, folding, in its own key order. JSON Lines. The native tools' `as a | b | c:` field lines as a table, with the lines around them as text. Tab and comma separated tables. Columns under a header, such as `ps`, `df`, and `lsof`. `key: value` and `key=value` blocks with their nesting. Unified diffs. Lists of paths. Markdown, through the replies' Markdown views. Anything else, or anything that only nearly fits a shape, is plain text. Terminal color codes are removed, carriage return progress shows its last state, binary data shows control characters as symbols, and the shell tool's exit status, time limit, and stopped notes show as facts, not output.
  - A raw view shows exactly what was stored. Text has line numbers, a wrap toggle, and find with Command-F. There are copy controls for the input, the output, and the whole call.
  - Large output stays fast. Shapes are detected on the first 64 KB, the layout runs off the main thread, text is drawn by a standard text view that lays out only what is in view, tables show at most 5,000 rows, and output over 4 MB is plain text.
- Showing and hiding
  - The hotkey and a left click on the menu bar icon do the same thing. When the panel is hidden, they show it with keyboard focus. When it is showing but another app has focus, the first press gives it focus, so the owner can type straight away. A press while it has focus hides it.
  - The panel stays up when the owner clicks or switches to another app. It hides only on the hotkey, the menu bar icon, the top bar's close button, Hide Panel in the top bar's menu, Escape, or Command-W. It never takes focus on its own.
  - Staying open and staying on top are separate. The panel is above other windows when it is summoned and while it, or its inspector, has focus. Once the owner clicks another window or switches apps, it layers like an ordinary window, so that window covers it, and it stays open underneath. A summon brings it back to the front. It never comes forward by itself when other windows change.
    - The mechanism is the level. The panel is at the floating level while it has focus and at the normal level without it. It is raised before it is ordered in on a summon. When it drops, it is ordered below the window that took focus, the front window of the app the owner switched to, because a change of level alone leaves it in front of that window.
    - It joins every Space and a full screen app's Space at either level, through its collection behavior. It has focus whenever it is summoned, so a summon over a full screen app floats as before.
  - The app's own windows always win. When Settings, a sheet or alert on it, or a file panel takes focus, the panel drops below it, in every preset and with Keep on Top on or off. It stays down while that window is open, so a Finder window Settings reveals is not covered either, until the panel takes focus again or the window closes.
  - Keep on Top, a checkable item under the presets in the top bar's menu, keeps the panel above other apps' windows without focus, as before this change. It is off by default and kept in the preferences. It is one setting for every preset, so switching layouts never changes how the panel layers. A quiet pin in the top bar shows that it is on, and clicking it turns it off.
  - The inspector is a child window and always has the panel's level. The incognito window follows the same rules and follows Keep on Top, which says only "keep panels on top" and so is not a trace. It shows the pin but cannot change the setting, since it writes nothing to the preferences.
  - The decision is `PanelLayering` in `MacOSAgentCore`.
  - A right click or Control-click on the icon opens the menu. So does activating the icon from the keyboard or with VoiceOver, and the menu's first item shows or hides the panel. VoiceOver also lists both as actions on the icon.
- Progress and dismissal
  - Each command shows as it runs, and the reply streams into place.
  - The go-ahead card sits by the text field, outside the scrolling history. The Stop button is on the working row at the end of the thread, inside it.
  - Hiding the panel never stops a task. The menu bar icon shows when a task is running or waiting for a go-ahead. The Stop button, Command-Period, and a menu item end it.
  - Command-Period stops the task of the window in front, the main panel or the incognito window, through Stop in the main menu, which is enabled only while that window's task runs. With no task it does nothing. Escape still hides the panel and never stops a task.
  - Stopping kills a running command's whole process group, with SIGTERM and then SIGKILL two seconds later, and cancels a model request in flight. A go-ahead that Stop ends reads as stopped, not as the owner declining. Every call the model made gets a result before the task ends, so the thread is valid for the next request, and the conversation says Stopped.
- A stable signing identity
  - The build script signs with the identity `MAC_OS_AGENT_SIGNING_IDENTITY` names, else a Developer ID Application certificate in the keychain, else a self-signed certificate named `mac-os-agent development`, else ad hoc. Each of the first three is the same app to macOS from build to build, so permission grants survive a rebuild. Moving from one identity to another is a new app to macOS once, and it asks again.
  - With a Developer ID the app has the hardened runtime and the entitlements in `Resources/mac-os-agent.entitlements`: Apple events, and Calendar, which also covers Reminders. It has no sandbox.
  - Where the sign-in and other secrets, such as a Brave Search key, are kept depends on the build. The rule is `SecretStorage` in `MacOSAgentCore`, and Debugging, Sign-in says which one the running build uses and why. A development build has no sign-in, since it reaches its models through inference-gateway, so its file holds only the other secrets.
    - The registration (the issued client ID and the account's `sub` and email) and the tokens (the refresh token and the ID token) are under separate keys in the same store, so signing out removes the tokens and keeps the registration.
    - A release build, the one `release.sh` makes and the only one with an update feed, keeps it in the login Keychain. It is signed with a Developer ID, so it has a team identifier, and macOS lets each update open the items an earlier one made without asking.
    - A development build keeps its secrets in `secrets.json` in the data folder, readable only by the owner. The Keychain asked twice after every rebuild, because without an Apple team identifier it ties "Always Allow" to the exact build. The cost is that any process running as the owner can read the file.
    - A run with `--data-folder` keeps it in that folder's `secrets.json` in any build and never touches the Keychain. Previews and tests never touch it either.
    - The items are generic passwords under the service `dev.lubelski.mac-os-agent`, one per key, in the login keychain, not the data protection keychain, so no entitlement or provisioning profile is needed. They are never synchronized to iCloud Keychain. Items under that service from before the sign-in moved to a file are ignored, and replaced when the same key is written.
    - The first time a release build launches with nothing in the Keychain and a sign-in in the default data folder's `secrets.json`, it moves every key there and removes them from the file. The log and Debugging say so, with a count and no secret. If the Keychain cannot be written, the file is left alone and that launch uses it, and Debugging says why. Once the Keychain has the sign-in, the file is left alone, so a development build on the same Mac keeps its own. Nothing moves from the Keychain to the file.
  - The certificates live in the owner's login keychain. They are machine setup and not in this repo.
- Releases and updates
  - A release build updates itself with Sparkle, the way Claude FM does. Sparkle is the one dependency besides the system.
  - `scripts/release.sh <version>` builds from `main`, signs with the Developer ID, notarizes, and publishes a GitHub Release tagged `v<version>` with `mac-os-agent-<version>.zip` and `appcast.xml`. `--dry-run` builds both into `build/release` and publishes nothing.
    - The version is the tag. The build number is the commit count, and Sparkle compares build numbers.
    - Notarization uses an App Store Connect API key when one is set up, in `NOTARY_KEY_FILE`, `NOTARY_KEY_ID`, and `NOTARY_ISSUER_ID` or in `~/.appstoreconnect/notary.conf`. It works with the screen locked, so the release poller can release with no one at the Mac. Without it the script uses the `AIMeter` keychain profile, which macOS does not open while the screen is locked.
    - `scripts/release.sh --check` changes nothing and exits 0 only when this Mac can release now: the Developer ID identity, the notarization credentials, the Sparkle public key, and the gh login. The release poller runs it as its preflight.
    - The notarization login, the `AIMeter` profile, and the Sparkle signing key are shared with the owner's other apps. The public key is in `scripts/sparkle-public-key.txt`.
    - Installed copies read the feed from the latest release without signing in, so the release repository has to be public. `MAC_OS_AGENT_RELEASE_REPO` names it, `lubelski/mac-os-agent` by default.
  - Only a build with a feed updates itself. `bundle.sh` writes `SUFeedURL` and `SUPublicEDKey` only when `release.sh` passes them, so a development build has no updater, no Check for Updates item, and no Update automatically switch.
  - It checks about once a day, downloads a newer build, checks its signature, and relaunches into it as soon as it is ready, not at the next quit.
    - The relaunch waits while a task runs or waits for a go-ahead in either window, while either window has queued messages, and while the incognito window holds a conversation, since those live only in memory. It goes ahead when the last of them ends. The rule is `AppUpdates` in `MacOSAgentCore`.
  - Settings, General has an Updates section with Update automatically, the version and build, and Check for Updates. The menu bar menu has Check for Updates. A build with no feed shows only the version and says it does not update itself.

- Habits from terminal coding agents
  - The owner uses Claude Code, pi, and Codex all day, so the panel's text field can follow their habits. Each is a switch in Settings, Behaviors, with a line saying exactly what the keys do. They are kept in the preferences by `HarnessBehavior`, and apply in both windows. The incognito window reads them and never writes them.
  - Up Arrow recalls earlier messages. On by default. Up with the insertion point on the field's first line, as laid out, or in an empty field, puts the owner's previous message in the field, and each press goes further back. Down on the last line comes forward, and past the newest brings back the draft that was being typed when recall started. After Up the insertion point is at the start, so Up again goes further back, and after Down it is at the end. Anywhere else in a multi-line draft the arrows move the insertion point as usual.
    - The history is the owner's messages in the window's history, across episodes, newest first, with consecutive repeats collapsed, up to 200. It includes a skill's slash command line and the typed text of a quick action's run, as they are kept. The app's own commands and instant app launches are not kept, so they are not recalled. The incognito window recalls only its own conversation, and clearing it forgets them.
    - The command list, the instant action row with several matches, and the open inspector in an empty field keep Up and Down. Recall gets the key only when they pass on it.
    - An edit to a recalled message is kept while moving through the others, as in readline. Sending it sends the edit as a new message. Sending, clearing, or Control-C ends recall.
  - Control-C stops or clears. Off by default. With a task running it stops it, as Command-Period does. With none running it clears a non-empty field, and with an empty field it does nothing. Command-C still copies.
  - Delete twice starts a new episode. On by default. Two presses of Delete in quick succession, with the field already empty, do what `/new` does, through the same path: a new episode in the main panel, a cleared conversation in the incognito window, nothing on a page that is already fresh beyond what `/new` would do, and while a task runs nothing, or with the queue experiment on the line saying `/new` can be used when the task ends. The first press does nothing visible.
    - Quick means within the system's double-click interval, the Mac's own setting for how close two presses are, read at each press.
    - Only two distinct presses both made on an already empty field count. A key repeat from holding Delete never counts, so holding it to erase a long draft does not start an episode when the field runs empty. Deleting the last character and pressing once more is one press on empty, not two.
    - Any other key, a click, a change to the text, the field losing focus, or the window hiding or showing again in between starts the count over. Delete while an input method is composing is the input method's.

    - The model is `PaneStackPath` in `MacOSAgentCore`: the item ids opened above a base pane, any number of them. It opens an item from any depth (replacing what was above it), goes back one, and goes back to a depth. It does not say where the base is, so the sidebar could become its first entry later. The sidebar stays separate for now.
    - The view is `PaneStackView`. A section gives it the view for each level, each covered strip's width, and each pane's title. A pane is usually a `StackPane`, a title pinned above a faint line with content that scrolls under it, and its rows are `PaneStackLink`s, which open their item one level deeper.
    - Each pane sits 10 points below the one under it, on a card with a rounded top-leading corner and a shadow to the left. A covered pane is dimmed, does not scroll or take clicks, is hidden from accessibility apart from a "Back to …" button, and keeps its scroll place. The open item's row stays marked.
    - A click on a covered pane's strip goes back to that pane. A click on a different row in the strip opens that row's item instead, replacing everything above. Escape closes one pane. With only the base showing, Escape does what it did before.
    - When the strips would leave the top pane less than 320 points, older strips give way first: each narrows to a 24-point sliver, oldest first, then slivers go away, oldest first, and only then does the strip just under the top pane shrink. A pane drops below the one under it only when that one's strip shows. `PaneStackLayout` holds the rule.
    - Panes slide with a 0.28-second spring with no bounce. With Reduce Motion they fade.
    - Picking another item from the same list while one is open is a swap, not a slide (owner, MAC-43: the side slide out and back in "is a bit much" for swapping one item for another). The pane's card, strip, and shadow stay put, and only its content changes: the old content fades out while moving 16 points one way and the new fades in from 16 points the other way, in 0.2 seconds on an ease out. An item lower in the list brings the new content up from below, and one higher brings it down from above. When either item is missing from the list, the swap only fades. Panes above the swapped one close with a fade rather than sliding out, so a click on another group while a tool's details are open reads as one change. With Reduce Motion on, a swap is a plain fade. Opening a pane and going back keep the side slide.
    - `PaneStackChange` in `MacOSAgentCore` tells an open, a back, and a swap apart from the path before and after, and gives a swap's direction from the order of the list it was picked from, which a section gives the view as `rows`. Tools' order is `rows(of:)` on its groups. Every duration, bounce, and distance the stack uses is in `PaneStackMotion`, also in `MacOSAgentCore`, with its defaults, for tuning later (MAC-48).
    - The stack and each pane's scroll place are kept in the section's model, so leaving the section and coming back finds them as they were while the window is open. They are not kept across launches.

A group opens its tools with 200 points of the groups showing. A tool opens its details, and the strips narrow to the groups' icons (56 points) and the start of the tools' names (170 points). The details are what the detail pane showed before, with the MCP go-ahead switch.
